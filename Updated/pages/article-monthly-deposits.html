Compound Interest

How monthly deposits affect your results

Monthly deposits change an investment projection in two ways: they increase the money contributed and give that money time to earn interest. The yearly results help distinguish those effects.

By Alysha Pursley Practical guide

Monthly contributions can have a substantial effect on a long-term projection. They increase the amount you invest and, because they enter the balance over time, can also increase the interest calculated in later months.

Airgead compares a starting balance that grows on its own with the same balance receiving regular monthly deposits. Both scenarios use the same assumed rate and timeframe, making the effect of contributions easier to see.

Separate contributions from interest

Principal is the money you put in: the starting amount plus all deposits. Interest is the additional amount produced by the calculation. The projected final balance includes both.

For example, starting with $1,000 and depositing $100 each month adds $1,200 over one year. Total principal is therefore $2,200 before considering interest.

Separating these amounts prevents a common misunderstanding. A larger final balance may reflect additional deposits rather than a higher return on the money invested.

When deposits begin earning interest

Airgead converts the assumed annual percentage rate into a monthly rate. In the contribution scenario, each deposit is added before that month’s interest is calculated.

The monthly rate is the annual percentage rate divided by 100 and then by 12. Each month, the calculation adds the deposit to the current balance and applies the monthly interest rate to that total.

Earlier deposits remain in the calculation for more months than later deposits. As a result, the timing of contributions affects how much interest they can earn under the model.

  • Start with the current balance.
  • Add the month's contribution in the deposit scenario.
  • Calculate interest using the updated balance.
  • Carry the resulting balance into the next month and repeat.

Use the year-by-year results

The Results page records the starting point and a summary at the end of every year. Each row reports the balance, principal, and interest, allowing you to see where the growth comes from.

The chart helps you see the overall direction of the two projections. Use the tables when you need to compare a particular year or check the reported figures.

The calculation carries balances forward month by month and displays currency values rounded to cents. Small differences may arise if you try to reproduce the results by repeatedly rounding intermediate values.

Compare one assumption at a time

On Compare, enter two scenarios with the same initial balance, rate, and timeframe. Change only the monthly deposit to isolate its effect on the projected result.

Next, hold the deposit constant and change the number of years. You can then test a different assumed rate. Comparing one change at a time makes the results easier to interpret.

The comparison illustrates how assumptions affect a mathematical projection. It does not identify the best investment or establish which return is achievable.

  • Compare $100 and $200 monthly contributions using the same rate and timeframe.
  • Keep the deposit constant and compare shorter and longer timeframes.
  • Use a lower assumed rate to see how the projection changes.
  • Look at both principal and earned interest, not only the final balance.

Limits of the projection

The model assumes a fixed rate and regular deposits. It excludes fees, taxes, inflation, losses, withdrawals, and changes in contribution amounts or interest rates.

Use the results to understand the relationship between contributions, time, and assumed interest—not as a promise of what an investment will earn.
