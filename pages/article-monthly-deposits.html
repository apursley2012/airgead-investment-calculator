Compound Interest

What monthly deposits actually change.

Compound interest matters, but the amount contributed along the way can matter just as much. Looking at both scenarios together makes that difference easier to understand.

By Alysha Pursley8-minute read

When people first learn about compound interest, the explanation usually focuses on money earning interest and then earning additional interest on the interest that has already accumulated. That definition is accurate, but it can still feel abstract. A percentage and a distant future balance do not always show what is happening between the starting point and the final result.

The Airgead Investment Calculator addresses that problem by running two projections at the same time. The first allows the initial balance to grow without any additional deposits. The second uses the same starting balance, interest rate, and timeframe but adds a recurring monthly contribution. Comparing those projections makes it possible to separate passive account growth from the effect of consistent saving.

One starting point, two different paths

Both scenarios begin with the same initial investment. They also use the same annual interest rate and the same number of years. The only difference is whether a monthly deposit is added. Because the remaining inputs stay consistent, the final comparison isolates the effect of recurring contributions instead of mixing several changes together.

The scenario without deposits answers a basic question: what happens if the original amount is left alone to grow? The deposit scenario answers a different question: what happens when the account continues receiving new principal every month? The distance between those two balances grows over time because each contribution affects the account in two ways. It directly increases the principal, and it creates another amount that can earn interest during the remaining months and years.

The difference is not created by interest alone.
The larger balance includes the money contributed each month plus the growth earned on those contributions.

Why timing affects the result

A deposit made early in a projection has more time to earn interest than one made near the end. That does not make later deposits unimportant. It means each contribution has a different amount of time to participate in the compounding process.

Airgead calculates growth in monthly steps. The annual rate is converted to a monthly rate by dividing it by 100 and then by 12. During each month, the current balance earns interest before the recurring deposit is added. Repeating that process twelve times produces the year-end balance shown in the results table.

This monthly model is especially useful for an educational calculator because it follows the same rhythm as many real household budgets. Income, bills, and savings contributions are often managed monthly. The interface connects that familiar schedule to a longer-term projection without asking the user to work through the formula manually.

Principal and interest need to be viewed separately

A large final balance can look impressive, but it does not explain how much of the total came from contributions and how much came from investment growth. For that reason, the results page reports total principal and earned interest separately.

Total principal includes the original balance and every monthly deposit added during the selected timeframe. Earned interest is calculated by subtracting that principal from the final balance with deposits. This distinction prevents the projection from presenting all growth as if it came from interest.

For example, increasing a monthly contribution may cause the final balance to rise sharply. Part of that increase is simply the additional money deposited. The rest is the interest those deposits had time to earn. Seeing both values helps the user interpret the result accurately.

Consistency can be explored without pretending every situation is the same

The calculator demonstrates a mathematical relationship; it does not decide what a person should contribute. A monthly deposit that is realistic for one household may be impossible for another. The purpose of changing the deposit field is not to create a universal target. It is to let users test amounts that make sense for their own hypothetical scenario.

The comparison tool expands that idea by placing two plans side by side. A user can change the monthly deposit, rate, or timeframe and see how the final balances differ. This makes several useful questions easier to explore:

  • How much does a smaller contribution change the result over a longer period?
  • What happens when the contribution increases but the timeframe stays the same?
  • How does the same monthly deposit perform under two different assumed rates?
  • How much of each final balance is principal, and how much is earned interest?

These are not predictions or guarantees. They are controlled comparisons based on the values entered into the calculator. Keeping that limitation clear is important because an assumed interest rate does not represent every fee, tax, market change, or real-world account condition.

Why the year-by-year table matters

Only showing the final value would hide most of the process. The year-by-year breakdown shows how the balance, principal, and interest change throughout the projection. Early in the timeline, the difference may appear modest. Later, the combined effect of repeated deposits and accumulated interest becomes more visible.

The chart provides a quick visual comparison, while the table provides exact values. Both are necessary because users do not all interpret information in the same way. A chart makes the widening gap easy to recognize. A table makes it possible to inspect a specific year without estimating from a line.

The practical lesson behind the calculation

The central lesson is not that one specific contribution guarantees one specific result. The useful lesson is that long-term growth depends on more than a starting balance. Contribution amount, contribution frequency, time, and rate work together.

By changing one input at a time, users can see which part of the scenario produced the difference. That direct feedback supports the original educational purpose of the project: making fiscal responsibility and compound growth easier to understand through interaction instead of relying only on definitions.

Airgead turns the calculation into something users can test. The numbers become easier to evaluate because the application shows where the final balance came from, how it changed each year, and what recurring deposits added to the result.
