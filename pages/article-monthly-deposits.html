Compound Interest

What monthly deposits actually change.

Compound interest matters, but the amount contributed along the way can matter just as much. Comparing both scenarios makes the difference easier to understand.

By Alysha Pursley8-minute read

What could happen if you regularly added a small amount to your savings instead of relying only on the money already there? The answer is more than a larger pile of deposits. Each new contribution can also begin earning interest, which changes how the account grows over time.

Compound interest means that money earns interest on the original balance and on interest that has already accumulated. The Airgead Investment Calculator makes that process easier to see by running two projections at once. One leaves the starting balance untouched. The other uses the same starting balance, rate, and timeframe but includes a monthly deposit.

Looking at both paths together helps answer a practical question: how much of the difference comes from regularly adding money, and how much comes from the growth earned on those contributions?

One starting point, two different paths

Both projections begin with the same initial investment. They also use the same annual rate and the same number of years. The key difference is whether a monthly deposit is included.

Without monthly deposits
The original balance grows only through the interest it earns.
With monthly deposits
The balance receives new principal every month, and those contributions can earn interest too.

What if the initial investment is left untouched? In that scenario, growth comes only from interest. Now imagine adding money every month. Each contribution immediately increases the principal and then has time to earn interest during the rest of the projection. Over several years, the distance between those balances can become substantial.

The difference is not created by interest alone.
The larger balance includes the money contributed each month plus the growth earned on those contributions.

Why contribution timing matters

A contribution made near the beginning of a projection has more time to grow than one made during the final year. Later deposits still increase the balance, but they have fewer monthly compounding periods available.

Airgead handles this in monthly steps. It divides the annual percentage rate by 100 and then by 12 to produce a monthly decimal rate. The calculator applies that rate to the current balance before adding the new monthly deposit. It repeats the process twelve times to produce each year-end result.

That sounds technical, but the basic idea is simple:

  1. The existing balance earns one month of interest.
  2. The monthly contribution is added.
  3. The new balance moves into the next month.
  4. The cycle repeats throughout the selected timeframe.

This monthly schedule is useful because many people already manage income, bills, and savings that way. The calculator connects a familiar monthly contribution to a long-term result without requiring the user to calculate every step manually.

Understanding principal and interest separately

A large final balance does not tell the whole story. To interpret the result correctly, it is important to know how much came from personal contributions and how much came from investment growth. That is why the Results page reports total principal and earned interest separately.

Principal includes the initial investment plus every monthly contribution. Earned interest is the portion remaining after that principal is subtracted from the final balance. Separating the two values shows whether most of the projected balance came from deposits or from compound growth.

Consider a simple hypothetical example. Someone starts with $1,000 and deposits $100 per month for one year. Before considering interest, the total principal at the end of that year is $2,200:

  • $1,000 initial investment
  • $1,200 contributed through twelve monthly deposits
  • $2,200 total principal

If the final projected balance is higher than $2,200, the amount above it represents earned interest in this simplified model. The exact result depends on the rate and how the calculator applies each monthly step. The example is not a promise of growth; it simply shows how to separate deposited money from investment earnings.

Using the comparison tool to test realistic options

This calculator demonstrates how the inputs relate mathematically. It does not prescribe how much anyone should contribute. A sustainable monthly amount depends on income, required expenses, debt, emergency savings, and other financial goals.

The comparison feature lets users explore two plans without treating either one as a universal answer. Change one input at a time and ask questions such as:

  • What happens if I contribute less each month but continue for longer?
  • How does increasing the deposit affect principal and earned interest?
  • What changes when both plans use the same deposit but different assumed rates?
  • How much of each final balance came from deposits rather than interest?

These are hypothetical comparisons, not predictions or guarantees. The calculator uses the values entered and assumes the same rate throughout the projection. It does not model taxes, fees, inflation, withdrawals, market volatility, or changing returns.

Why year-by-year data matters

A final number provides a destination, but the yearly breakdown shows the path. During the first few years, the difference between two scenarios may appear small. As contributions continue and more of the balance has time to earn interest, the gap can become easier to see.

The chart provides a quick visual comparison. The table provides the exact balance, principal, and interest recorded for each year. Together, they make it possible to recognize the overall trend and inspect a specific point in the projection.

The practical takeaway

Long-term growth depends on several connected factors: the starting balance, contribution amount, contribution frequency, time, and assumed rate. Monthly deposits matter because they add principal consistently and give at least some of that new money time to earn interest.

Even so, a larger input is not automatically a better real-life plan if the amount cannot be maintained. The most useful projection begins with a contribution that fits the person’s actual budget. From there, Airgead can show how that amount behaves over time and how a different contribution would change the result.

Try adjusting one value at a time. Watch how the chart changes, then check the table to see where the difference came from. That interaction turns compound interest from an abstract definition into a process that can be examined year by year.
